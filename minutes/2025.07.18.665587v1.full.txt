Abstract
Tissue function is tightly linked to the spatial organization, and our knowledge of the relationship be-
tween tissue structure and function has increased due to the rapid technological development of spatial
transcriptomics approaches. A bottle-neck is, however, the analysis of the complex and high-dimensional
spatial transcriptomics data. Many computational tools have been proposed for spatial analysis but eval-
uating them remains challenging due to the scarcity of datasets with known ground truth. To address
this, we developed SimSpace , a flexible simulation framework that can generate synthetic spatial cell
maps with categorical cell type labels and biologically meaningful organization. Cell type spatial pat-
terns are simulated using a Markov Random Field model, enabling the control of spatial autocorrelation
and interaction between cell types. This approach captures a broad range of tissue architectures, from
well-separated niches to spatially mixed environments. We evaluate the simulated data using spatial
statistics metrics (eg. entropy, Moran’s I) and benchmark the results against real spatial transcriptomics
and proteomics datasets. Our findings show that the simulations closely mirror key spatial properties
observed in biological tissues. Additionally, we demonstrate the utility of our framework as a testbed for
benchmarking spatial gene detection and cell type deconvolution methods. By providing reproducible,
ground-truth-controlled datasets, SimSpace supports the development, validation, and comparison of
computational methods in spatial omics.
Introduction
Spatial omics technologies are rapidly advancing and have revealed that tissue function is intimately tied to
the spatial organization of cells. Technologies like spatial transcriptomics and other spatial omics techniques
have enabled the profiling of molecular features in their native spatial context, providing unprecedented
insights into tissue architecture and cellular interactions. Understanding these spatial patterns is crucial
for many biology questions, including tissue development, embryogenesis, neuroscience, and immunological
dynamics [1–4].
Many computational methods and tools have been developed to analyze spatial omics data [5, 6]. Hun-
dreds of methods, with many focusing on spatial omics data analysis, have been proposed for tasks like
deconvolution [7–11], spatial variable gene detection [12–17], spatial imputation [18, 19], and spatial domain
recognition [20–22]. Evaluating these tools, however, remains challenging due to the limited availability of
datasets with known ground truth.
In-silico simulations, in this case, offer a promising solution by generating realistic synthetic spatial omics
datasets for benchmarking method performance. Currently, many methodology studies are using their simu-
lation settings either too simplistic [23–26] or biased by assumptions that are too specific [14, 27,28], resulting
in a biased benchmarking result. Several emerging simulation tools have been designed for this task, and
these can be classified into two major types, the reference-based and reference-free (or de novo) approaches.

The reference-based approaches, including simulation tools like scDesign [29] and SRTsim [30], always re-
quire a spatial omics reference dataset to generate corresponding omics-level and spatial-level simulations,
which results in a simulated dataset that has almost identical spatial coordinates and patterns as the refer-
ence data. This limits the ability of reference-based tools to generate diverse spatial patterns and cell type
distributions, as the simulated datasets are constrained by the reference data.
In contrast, reference-free approaches such as scCube [31] and scMultiSim [32] rely on simplified assumptions
for generating spatial features. For example, scCube employs a basic Gaussian distribution to model cell
type spatial distributions in its reference-free mode, which does not adequately capture the intricate spatial
organization and heterogeneity observed in real tissues. This approach also lacks the ability to model spatial
heterogeneity and cell-cell interactions across distinct tissue regions or niches, which are essential for faithfully
representing tissue architecture [33]. Similarly, scMultiSim utilizes a straightforward time series model to
generate spatial patterns by simulating one cell at each time point, which may not sufficiently reflect the
complex spatial relationships among cells. Overall, these reference-free methods are limited in their ability
to recapitulate the biological complexity and cellular spatial proximity inherent in real tissue environments,
which is crucial for accurately simulating spatial omics data. Additionally, there is no good benchmark for
these reference-free simulation tools, making it difficult to systematically evaluate their performance and
biological realism.
To address these challenges, we present SimSpace, a comprehensive simulation framework for generating
synthetic spatial cell maps with biologically realistic organization. SimSpace adopts a hierarchical model-
ing strategy: it first simulates spatial cell distributions using a Markov Random Field approach, enabling
precise control over niche composition, spatial proximity, and cell-cell interactions. It then generates the
corresponding omics profiles, including transcriptomic and proteomic data, to support downstream analyses.
The framework supports both reference-free and reference-based simulation modes, allowing users to cre-
ate diverse tissue architectures ranging from well-defined niches to spatially intermixed environments across
resolutions ( Fig. 1A ). Moreover, SimSpace also supports three-dimensional simulations, capturing the full
complexity of tissue structure.
We systematically evaluate SimSpace using multiple spatial statistics and benchmark its outputs against real
datasets, including a Xenium [34] human breast tumor dataset (spatial transcriptomics) and a CODEX [ 35]
(also known as PhenoCycler-Fusion) human endometrial tumor dataset (spatial proteomics). These evalu-
ations demonstrate SimSpace’s utility for benchmarking computational methods such as cell type deconvo-
lution and spatially variable gene detection. SimSpace is implemented as an open-source Python package
with flexible parameterization and modular design, making it accessible for a wide range of spatial omics
research applications. Together, these features position SimSpace as a valuable resource for generating re-
alistic, ground-truth-controlled spatial datasets to support rigorous benchmarking and method development
in spatial omics.
Results
Overview of SimSpace
SimSpace is a comprehensive framework designed to simulate spatial omics data through a hierarchical
modeling strategy ( Supp Fig. 1). For the spatial component, SimSpace first generates spatial niches
within a defined spatial grid, capturing spatial autocorrelation and niche-level interactions. Subsequently,
within each niche, single-cell phenotypes are simulated to model cell–cell interaction patterns. Additional
simulation parameters, such as cell densities and spatial perturbations, are incorporated to generate the final
spatial coordinates of individual cells (Fig. 1B). For the omics component, SimSpace simulates cellular
omics profiles based on spatial coordinates and cell type assignments, utilizing either built-in probabilistic
models or external simulation tools. The resulting output is a spatial omics dataset comprising spatial
coordinates, cell type labels, and corresponding omics profiles. This dataset can be used for a variety of
downstream applications, including the validation and benchmarking of computational methods such as cell
type deconvolution, spatial variable gene detection, and spatial clustering.
SimSpace’s simulation framework is built upon Markov Random Field (MRF) models, which are widely
used probabilistic graphical models for capturing spatial dependencies among random variables arranged on
a lattice or grid [36–38]. In the context of spatial omics data, MRFs enable the modeling of local interactions,
as each variable (e.g., cell phenotypes) is conditionally dependent only on its immediate neighbors rather
than on distant elements (Fig. 2A ), thereby reflecting the inherent spatial organization of biological tissues.

This approach is particularly well-suited for simulating spatial omics data, as it allows for the generation

simulation tools, SimSpace offers a flexible and extensible framework capable of generating both de novo
and reference-based spatial omics datasets with novel but biologically realistic spatial architectures. In the
absence of reference data, SimSpace can simulate spatial patterns using user-defined MRF parameters and
other key factors such as cell densities and spatial autocorrelation. Alternatively, when a reference spatial
omics dataset is available, SimSpace can infer model parameters to generate simulated spatial patterns that
are different but closely recapitulate the spatial features and biological relevance of the reference. This
versatility enables SimSpace to produce a broad spectrum of spatial omics datasets with varying resolutions,
spatial organizations, and complexities to support diverse research applications.
Reference-free simulation of spatial omics data
SimSpace enables reference-free simulation of spatial omics data, allowing users to generate synthetic datasets
with customizable spatial patterns and cell type distributions independent of any reference dataset. This
mode is particularly advantageous when reference data are unavailable or when there is a need to model
specific spatial architectures not observed in existing datasets. The reference-free simulation workflow begins
with the specification of key hyperparameters, including the number of spatial niches, cell types, the neigh-
borhood structure, and the spatial grid configuration. Using these parameters, SimSpace generates spatial
cell maps exhibiting diverse and biologically relevant spatial organizations (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, omics
profiles for individual cells are simulated in a reference-free manner ( Fig. 2C), utilizing either the built-in
Gamma-Poisson probabilistic model or external single-cell simulation tools such as splatter [39]. To incor-
porate spatial context into the simulated profiles, SimSpace offers an optional adjustment step based on a
spatial ligand-receptor interaction model, which modifies the expression levels according to cell positions and
phenotypes. Such interactions are known to play critical roles in spatial transcriptomics [40, 41]. This step
enhances the biological realism of the reference-free simulated data by coupling molecular variation to spatial
context. Collectively, the reference-free simulation mode provides a flexible platform for generating spatial
omics datasets with user-defined spatial and phenotypic characteristics, facilitating method development and
benchmarking in the absence of empirical references.
To evaluate the ability of SimSpace to generate spatial patterns that resemble those observed in real spatial
omics data, we created eight simulation datasets in reference-free mode using randomly generated parameters.
These simulated datasets were compared to a real spatial omics dataset, specifically the Xenium human
breast tumor dataset (Supp Fig. 2), by analyzing the cumulative distributions of Moran’s Index (MI)
across cell types. MI is a widely used metric for quantifying spatial autocorrelation and characterizing the
degree of spatial clustering among cell phenotypes within a sample [40, 42,43]. For each cell type in both
the simulated and real datasets, we computed MI values and compared their cumulative distributions. The
results demonstrate that SimSpace is capable of producing simulated datasets with MI distributions closely
matching those of the real dataset ( Fig. 2D), as indicated by a non-significant Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
(p-value = 0.0604 >0.05) between the SimSpace and Xenium datasets. For comparison, we also created
eight simulated datasets using scCube [31], a reference-free spatial simulation tool, and assessed their MI
distributions relative to the Xenium dataset. In this case, the MI distributions of scCube simulations differed
significantly from those of the real data (p-value = 5 .95×10−12), suggesting that scCube may not fully
capture the spatial characteristics of biological tissues. These results indicate that SimSpace can generate
spatial patterns that more accurately recapitulate the spatial organization observed in real spatial omics
data compared to existing reference-free simulation tools.
Three-dimensional spatial omics data simulation
SimSpace extends its simulation capabilities to three-dimensional spatial omics data, enabling the modeling
of tissue architectures with increased biological realism. In this mode, the simulation framework generalizes
the MRF model to three spatial dimensions, allowing users to specify a 3D spatial grid, including the number
of layers and the neighborhood structure in all three axes. Both reference-free and reference-based simulation
modes are supported, with spatial patterns, cell type distributions, and omics profiles generated in a manner
analogous to the two-dimensional case. The resulting 3D spatial omics datasets capture complex spatial
relationships and heterogeneity inherent to volumetric tissues (Fig. 2E,F). This functionality facilitates
the benchmarking and development of computational methods designed for three-dimensional spatial omics

Reference-based simulation of spatial omics data
SimSpace further enables reference-based simulation of spatial omics data, allowing users to generate syn-
thetic spatial datasets that closely recapitulate the spatial organization of a given reference sample through
an optimization-driven approach ( Fig. 3A ). In this mode, SimSpace first simulates spatial patterns by
minimizing the contrastive loss between key spatial statistics, specifically Moran’s Index and neighborhood
entropy, computed for the simulated and reference datasets. The optimization employs a genetic algorithm
to iteratively refines the parameters of the MRF model, including both niche-level and cell-type interaction
matrices, as well as additional relevant parameters, to achieve the best match to the reference. Preservation
of these spatial statistics ensures that the simulated dataset retains the essential biological features of the
reference. At the local scale, neighborhood entropy quantifies the diversity of cell types within the immediate
vicinity of each cell, while at the global scale, Moran’s Index measures spatial autocorrelation of cell type
distributions. By first computing these metrics from the reference dataset and then optimizing the simulation
parameters accordingly, SimSpace produces spatial patterns that faithfully reflect the spatial characteristics
of the original sample.
When omics data are available in the reference dataset, SimSpace can generate corresponding omics profiles
by leveraging the simulated spatial patterns through various built-in or external simulation tools that enables
the multimodal single-cell data simulation, such as scDesign3 [29]. The resulting simulated omics profiles
are then integrated with the generated spatial coordinates and cell type annotations to produce the final
spatial omics dataset.
To evaluate the performance of SimSpace in reference-based simulation, we selected a 1mm ×1mm tile from
the Xenium human breast tumor dataset [34] as the reference and generated a corresponding simulated
dataset using SimSpace (Fig. 3B). For comparison, we also generated a simulated dataset using scCube,
which produces omics profiles in a reference-based manner but generates spatial patterns using its reference-
free approach. We hypothesized that an effective simulation should accurately recapitulate key biological
features of the reference dataset, such as cell type annotations, and exhibit no significant differences from
the reference in downstream analyses.
To assess this, we applied several spatial clustering methods, including BANKSY [21], SpatialPCA [22],
and SEDR [44], to the reference and both simulated datasets. The clustering results demonstrated that
SimSpace-generated data were closely aligned with the reference, showing minimal batch effects, whereas
scCube simulations exhibited a clear batch effect ( Fig. 3C, Supp Fig. 3 ). To further investigate whether
these differences were attributable to the omics data or the spatial patterns, we replaced the simulated omics
profiles in both SimSpace and scCube datasets with the corresponding Xenium reference data, matching cell
types at random. Spatial clustering performed on these modified datasets yielded results consistent with the
original analysis, indicating that the observed batch effect in scCube is primarily due to differences in spatial
patterns rather than omics profiles (Supp Fig. 4). Together, these results demonstrate that SimSpace can
faithfully reproduce the spatial organization of the reference dataset, generating spatial omics simulations
that closely resemble the original data—a level of fidelity not achieved by other reference-based simulation
tools.
Benchmarking computational methods using SimSpace simulated datasets
In contrast to existing simulation tools, SimSpace enables the generation of an unlimited number of spatial
omics datasets with customizable spatial patterns and properties. This flexibility positions SimSpace as a
powerful in silico benchmarking platform for spatial omics computational methods. By producing a diverse
array of simulated datasets with known ground truth, SimSpace facilitates rigorous evaluation and compar-
ison of computational approaches under controlled and reproducible conditions. To illustrate its utility, we
apply SimSpace for benchmarking two key spatial omics analysis tasks: cell type deconvolution and spatial
variable gene detection.
Cell type deconvolution

relative abundances of distinct cell types within each spatial location, particularly in datasets with multi-
cellular resolution [45, 46]. In this study, we systematically evaluated five widely used cell type deconvolution
methods: cell2location [7], RCTD (in both ’full’ and ’multi’ modes) [8], CARD [9], Seurat [10], and spa-

To systematically benchmark these methods, we employed SimSpace to generate a series of reference-based
simulated datasets derived from randomly selected tiles of the Xenium human breast tumor dataset. To
capture a broad range of biological and spatial diversity, we varied both the number of cell types and the
spatial distribution patterns for each reference tile, resulting in 16 distinct simulated datasets ( Supp Fig.
5). Each simulated dataset was further processed by applying four different convolution kernel sizes, thereby
modeling a range of spatial resolutions. The deconvolution methods were then applied to these convolved
datasets, and their performance was assessed by comparing the predicted cell type proportions to the ground
truth proportions from the original SimSpace simulations ( Fig. 4A). Evaluation metrics included Pearson
correlation coefficient (PCC), root mean square error (RMSE), and Jaccard Index (JI), were calculated for
each convolved spot to quantitatively assess the accuracy of cell type proportion estimates.
The benchmarking results indicate that most deconvolution methods achieve high Pearson correlation coeffi-
cients (PCC) with the ground truth cell type proportions, with RCTD and CARD consistently demonstrating
the highest accuracy ( Fig. 4B, C, Supp Fig. 6). Comparable trends are observed for the root mean
square error (RMSE) metric. Notably, while most methods exhibit improved performance with increasing
convolution kernel sizes, Seurat and spatialDWLS perform optimally at smaller kernel sizes, suggesting that
these methods may be more sensitive to spatial resolution and potentially less suitable for datasets with
larger spot sizes, such as Visium. To further assess the ability of each method to detect the presence of
specific cell types, we evaluated performance using the Jaccard Index (JI). Consistent with the PCC and
RMSE results, Seurat and spatialDWLS achieve the highest JI at smaller kernel sizes, whereas other methods
show increased JI with larger kernel sizes. Additionally, we investigated the impact of cell type complexity
by varying the number of cell types in the simulated datasets from 8 to 14. The results reveal that all
methods tend to perform better with fewer cell types ( Fig. 4D ), which is expected given the increased
complexity and heterogeneity associated with a greater number of cell types, making accurate deconvolution
more challenging.
In summary, our benchmarking of cell type deconvolution methods using SimSpace-simulated datasets
demonstrates that most approaches are capable of accurately recovering cell type proportions across a range
of spatial resolutions and complexities. Among the evaluated methods, RCTD and CARD consistently
exhibited the highest accuracy across varying kernel sizes. Additionally, we observed that increasing the
number of cell types in the dataset led to a reduction in deconvolution accuracy for all methods, reflecting
the greater challenge posed by higher cellular complexity. These results underscore the value of SimSpace
for generating controlled, ground-truth datasets that enable systematic and rigorous evaluation of spatial
deconvolution algorithms under diverse experimental conditions.
Spatial variable gene detection
Spatial variable gene (SVG) detection is another common task in spatial transcriptomics data analysis, where
the goal is to identify genes that exhibit non-random, informative spatial patterns in the sample [49, 50].
In this study, we benchmarked six different spatial variable gene detection methods, including Hotspot [12],
SpatialDE [13], scBSP [14, 15], SPARK [16], Giotto [17], and Moran’s I implemented in the Seurat pack-
age [51].
To systematically benchmark these spatial variable gene detection methods, we utilized the Xenium human
breast tumor dataset as a reference to generate SimSpace datasets under both reference-based and reference-
free simulation modes (Fig. 5A ). For the reference-based scenario, we generated a simulated dataset that
closely recapitulates the spatial patterns and gene expression profiles of the Xenium reference. Each SVG
detection method was then applied to both the simulated and reference datasets, enabling direct compar-
ison of their outputs and assessment of method reproducibility. In parallel, we generated eight additional
simulated datasets using reference-free spatial patterns while maintaining reference-based gene expression
profiles. SVG detection methods were applied to these datasets to evaluate the robustness of each approach
and to investigate the impact of varying spatial patterns on SVG detection performance.
Different SVG detection methods employ distinct statistical models and underlying assumptions, resulting in
p-values with varying scales and distributions. To enable rigorous and unbiased comparison across methods,
we implemented a rank-based normalization procedure: for each method, p-values were ranked from lowest
to highest and then mapped onto a common scale [52]. This approach facilitates direct comparison of results
across both methods and datasets. Using the reference-based simulated dataset, we assessed the concordance

spatial patterns. Hotspot also demonstrated substantial concordance, while the remaining methods showed
comparatively lower levels of agreement. These results suggest that scBSP and Hotspot are more robust
to changes in spatial organization, whereas other methods may be more sensitive to the underlying spatial
structure of the data.

datasets demonstrates that most approaches yield highly reproducible and robust results when compared to
real spatial omics data. Notably, methods such as scBSP and Hotspot exhibited the highest levels of con-
sistency and robustness to variations in spatial patterns, whereas others, including SpatialDE and Moran’s
I, were more sensitive to changes in spatial structure. The application of rank-based normalization to p-
values facilitated equitable comparison across methods and datasets, revealing that the majority of evaluated
approaches can reliably identify spatially informative genes under both reference-based and reference-free
simulation scenarios. Together, these findings highlight the utility of SimSpace for generating controlled,
ground-truth spatial omics datasets, thereby enabling systematic evaluation and comparison of SVG detec-
tion algorithms and supporting the advancement of spatial analysis methodologies.
SimSpace for spatial proteomics data simulation
Besides spatial transcriptomics, SimSpace can also be applied to simulate spatial proteomics datasets, which
is important for understanding the spatial organization and tissue environment [53]. By incorporating protein
expression data and spatial information, SimSpace can generate realistic spatial proteomics datasets that
reflect the complex interactions between different cell types in their microenvironment. To demonstrate this,
we applied SimSpace to a selected tile of a CODEX (CO-Detection by indEXing) human endometrial tumor
dataset, which contains spatial proteomics data with 31 protein markers and 206 cells with 9 phenotypes.
CODEX is a highly multiplexed imaging technology that enables simultaneous detection of dozens of protein
markers at single-cell resolution within intact tissue sections, where data are collected by iterative cycles
of antibody staining, imaging, and fluorophore removal, allowing for comprehensive spatial profiling of the
tumor microenvironment [54]. We generated a reference-based simulated dataset based on the reference tile
from the CODEX dataset, and then applied SimSpace to simulate the spatial patterns of the tumor samples
and the corresponding protein marker expression profiles in the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 6A ).
By leveraging the MRF model, SimSpace encodes the spatial organization of the tissue into the potential
parameters, which can be interpreted as spatial proximity between different cell types (Fig. 6B). A positive
potential indicates a strong attraction between two cell types, while a negative potential indicates spatial
exclusion. For example, the parameter matrix inferred from the CODEX reference dataset reveals strong

Discussion
SimSpace fills a critical gap in spatial omics research by enabling the systematic generation of synthetic
datasets with known ground truth, controllable spatial organization, and flexible omics profiles. Our results
demonstrate that SimSpace’s MRF-based modeling approach can recapitulate a wide spectrum of tissue
architectures, from highly structured niches to spatially intermixed environments, and accurately repro-
duce key spatial statistics like Moran’s Index and neighborhood entropy. This fidelity is evident in both
reference-free and reference-based simulation modes, where SimSpace outperforms existing tools like scCube
in matching the spatial properties of real datasets, as shown by statistical comparisons with Xenium spatial
transcriptomics data.
A major strength of SimSpace is its hierarchical simulation framework, which decouples spatial niche gen-
eration, cellular spatial pattern modeling, and omics profile simulation. This modularity allows users to
independently control spatial autocorrelation, cellular spatial proximity, cell density, and marker gene ex-
pression, or to fit these parameters to a reference dataset. The ability to simulate three-dimensional spatial
omics data further extends SimSpace’s applicability to emerging 3D spatial technologies, supporting the
benchmarking of methods that require volumetric tissue context. Through comprehensive benchmarking,
we demonstrated that SimSpace-generated datasets provide a rigorous and reproducible platform for eval-
uating computational methods. In cell type deconvolution, SimSpace enabled the systematic assessment
of multiple algorithms across varying spatial resolutions and cell type complexities, revealing that methods
such as RCTD and CARD are robust to changes in spatial scale, while others are more sensitive to kernel
size or cell type diversity. For spatial variable gene (SVG) detection, SimSpace allowed us to compare the
reproducibility and robustness of six widely used methods under both reference-based and reference-free
scenarios. Our results highlight that while most SVG methods are consistent across simulated and real data,
some (e.g., scBSP and Hotspot) are more robust to changes in spatial pattern, whereas others are more
sensitive to spatial structure.
Importantly, SimSpace’s application to spatial proteomics data, as demonstrated with the CODEX human
endometrial tumor dataset, illustrates its versatility beyond transcriptomics. By inferring and encoding cell-
cell interaction matrices from reference data, SimSpace not only simulates realistic spatial architectures but
also provides interpretable parameters that can reveal underlying biological organization, such as immune
cell exclusion or stromal-endothelial co-localization in the tumor microenvironment.
Despite these advances, several limitations should be noted. First, the current implementation of SimSpace
primarily focuses on spatial omics data simulation, and while it can generate corresponding omics profiles, the
accuracy of these profiles depends on the underlying probabilistic models or external simulation tools used.
Future work will aim to enhance the omics simulation models, potentially incorporating additional biological
knowledge such as gene regulatory networks or signaling pathways to improve the fidelity of simulated
profiles. Second, while SimSpace supports both reference-free and reference-based simulation modes, the
latter requires a reference dataset to guide the spatial pattern generation. This may limit its applicability
in scenarios where such references are not available or where users wish to explore novel spatial patterns not
present in existing data. In such cases, the reference-free mode can still be used, but the spatial patterns
may not fully reflect the complexity of target real biological tissues and may require careful parameter
tuning to achieve realistic results. Finally, while SimSpace provides a powerful framework for simulating
and representing spatial omics data, it is important to recognize that no simulation tool can fully capture

methods, and supports deeper exploration of spatial organization in complex tissues.

To simulate the spatial distribution of cell phenotypes

Markov Random Field models for the spatial model-

random variables arranged on a lattice or grid, which
has been widely used in image analysis and computer
vision where spatial information is crucial [37, 59].We model the spatial configuration of cell phenotypes
as a Markov Random Field (MRF) defined over a
two-dimensional grid, where each grid location corre-
sponds to a cell position, and the cell phenotype at
each position is treated as a random variable. Specif-

In a reference-free scenario, the number of spatial
niches mand the number of cell phenotypes kwill be
firstly defined, together with the neighborhood struc-
tureNand the spatial gird L. The grid has a default
setting of a 100 ×100 square grid, while it can also be
customized to other shapes as long as each pixel rep-
resents a potential cell position. The neighborhood
structure N, customized by the maximal distance of
interaction pairs, functions during the MRF process,
where a larger neighborhood stands for a boarder cell-
cell interaction range.
Niche simulation. To simulate niches across
space, we applied a niche-level MRF model with
the given niche potential parameters Θ niche as the
transition matrix. Specifically, given the number
of spatial niches m, a symmetric matrix Θ m×m =
{θi,j|i, j∈ {1, . . . , m }}will be generated from a ran-
dom generator. Then, a Gibbs sampler is performed
to generate the niche distributions from the niche-
level MRF model.
Cell type simulation. Within each niche, the phe-
notype interaction scores will be generated to simu-
late the different cell-cell interaction patterns in dif-
ferent niches. For each niche, a symmetric matrix
Θn×n={θi,j|i, j∈ {1, . . . , n }}will then be generated
as well from the random generator. Another Gibbs
sampler will then be performed to simulate cells’ spa-
tial distributions.
Cell density and coordinates simulation. Next,
a cell density sampler will be applied to simulate the
different cell densities for each phenotype. The k
density parameters {d1, d2, . . . , d k}for each pheno-
type play as the expected fraction of cells kept in
the final simulation result. Finally, perturbations on
the cells’ coordinates are added to turn the gird-like
into continuous spatial coordinates. The perturba-
tion can be defined by a Gaussian distribution with
a user-defined standard deviation σperturb , which will
be added to the original coordinates of each cell.

Three-dimensional data simulation
To simulate three-dimensional spatial omics data, we
extend the two-dimensional MRF model to a three-
dimensional MRF model. The neighborhood struc-
tureNis defined in three dimensions, and the cor-
responding potential functions ζare defined in three
dimensions as well. Specifically, the potential func-
tion is still in the same form as equation 4, where now
the neighborhood Ninow includes all adjacent voxels
in three-dimensional space. The simulation process,
including Gibbs sampling and parameterization, re-
mains unchanged except for the extension to 3D co-
ordinates (x, y, z ). This allows SimSpace to gener-
ate realistic three-dimensional spatial omics datasets
with controllable spatial structure and cell-cell inter-
actions.
Spatial clustering and visualization
SpatialPCA and BANKSY are used to perform the
spatial clustering of simulated and real spatial omics
data. We first concatenate the spatial coordinates
and the omics data of SimSpace, scCube, and Xenium
reference data into a single dataset, and then perform
clustering methods on the concatenated matrix. For
spatialPCA, we selected top 100 highly variable genes
to perform PCA while keeping other parameters as
default. The first two principal components are then
used for the plotting. For BANKSY, we tested dif-
ferent λsetting, 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8, and visualized the
results using UMAP plots.
Additionally, to isolate the spatial effects from the
omics data, we replaced the simulated omics dataof SimSpace and scCube with the Xenium reference
data. Specifically, for each cell in SimSpace and sc-
Cube, we replaced the simulated omics data with
the corresponding Xenium reference data from a ran-
domly selected cell that has the same cell type. The
replaced omics data is then used for the spatial clus-
tering and visualization.

Figure 1
Figure 1: Overview of the SimSpace framework and its downstream applications. (A) The SimSpace frame-
work consists of two main components: the spatial pattern simulator and the omics profile simulator.
SimSpace supports both reference-free and reference-based simulation modes. A de novo spatial grid is
first initialized, upon which spatial parameters (e.g., spatial niches, cell density, and cell-cell interaction) are
simulated. These parameters are optionally fit to reference spatial datasets by optimizing spatial autocor-
relation metrics such as Moran’s index and neighborhood entropy. Simulated omics profiles are generated
from the single-cell simulation tools and integrated with spatial patterns to produce a spatial omics dataset.
The resulting datasets enable various downstream applications, including spatial clustering, cell type decon-
volution, spatially variable gene detection, spatial niche identification, and 3D spatial simulations. (B) A
SimSpace simulation example workflow. First, the niches are simulated using a niche-level Markov Random
Field model, which generates spatial niches with different interaction patterns. Then, the cell types are
simulated within each niche using a cell-level simulation model, which captures the interactions between
neighboring cell types. Next, cell densities are simulated, and a perturbation is applied to the cell coordi-
nates to generate a continuous spatial distribution eventually.

Supplementary Figure 1
Figure S1: Overview of the SimSpace generative framework. Flowchart summarizing the three main stages
of SimSpace simulation: (1) Spatial Pattern Simulation: A spatial grid is initialized, followed by user-defined
settings or fitted parameters from reference data for the number of cell types, neighborhood structure, and
niche/cell-type interactions. Markov Random Fields (MRFs) are simulated via Gibbs sampling to generate
structured spatial distributions. (2) Phenotype Simulation: Phenotype-level MRFs are constructed, followed
by density sampling and coordinate perturbation to emulate realistic cellular density and variability. (3)
Omics Data Simulation: Omics parameters are either fitted from a reference dataset or generated de novo.
Simulated spatial patterns are paired with expression profiles to produce final spatial omics datasets.

Figure S7: Comparison of SVG rankings between SimSpace simulations and the Xenium reference dataset.
Scatter plots compare gene ranks derived from SimSpace reference-based simulations versus the original
Xenium reference across seven SVG detection methods: scBSP, Hotspot, Giotto (kmeans), Giotto (rank),
Moran’s I, SPARK, and SpatialDE. Each point represents one gene; dotted red lines indicate linear regression
fit. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) are shown for each method. scBSP and Hotspot achieve the highest
concordance with the reference, while SpatialDE and Moran’s I exhibit lower agreement, suggesting greater
sensitivity to spatial variation.
