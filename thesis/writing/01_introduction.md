Spatial transcriptomics has made it possible to measure gene expression without removing cells from the tissue in which they reside, but many of the technologies in current use record the expression of small groups of cells rather than of individual cells. Recovering the cellular composition of each measurement has therefore become a central computational task, and a large number of deconvolution methods have been proposed to perform it, almost all of which rely on cell-type expression profiles obtained from single-cell RNA sequencing. Deciding which of these methods to trust requires benchmarks, and benchmarks require data whose true composition is known, which in practice means simulated data. This chapter introduces these elements in turn, from the single-cell data that define cell types to the spatial data that mix them, and from the methods that unmix them to the simulation studies that evaluate those methods. It then argues that the conclusions of such studies may depend on the source of the expression profiles assigned to each cell type, a choice that has recently been widened by generative models trained on public archives, and presents the question addressed in this thesis and the approach taken to answer it.

## 1.1 Single-cell transcriptomics and the definition of cell types

Single-cell RNA sequencing measures the transcriptome of thousands of individual cells in a single experiment. In the droplet-based protocols that dominate current practice, each cell is encapsulated with a barcoded bead, and each captured transcript is tagged with a unique molecular identifier (UMI), so that the data consist of a matrix of integer counts of molecules per gene and per cell. Because only a small fraction of the transcripts of each cell is captured, these matrices are sparse and their counts are overdispersed relative to a Poisson distribution; for droplet data with UMIs the negative binomial distribution describes them adequately without the need for additional zero inflation (Svensson 2020). In a standard analysis the cells are filtered for quality, normalised, grouped by unsupervised clustering and annotated as cell types on the basis of known marker genes (Heumos et al. 2023). The average expression of each gene within an annotated population, its mean expression profile, is the quantity through which single-cell data enter most of the analyses discussed in this thesis.

Two features of these data are relevant here. First, in tissues that are difficult to dissociate, such as the adult human brain, the transcriptome is often measured in isolated nuclei rather than whole cells. Nuclei contain only part of the cellular mRNA and yield fewer detected genes, yet closely related cell types can be discriminated with a resolution comparable to that of whole cells (Bakken et al. 2018). Second, cells of the same type differ between individuals, so that a profile estimated from one donor is only an approximation of the profile of the same cell type in another. Large collaborative efforts such as the Human Cell Atlas (Regev et al. 2017) and public repositories such as CZ CELLxGENE Discover, which distributes standardised count matrices with harmonised annotations of donor, tissue and cell type (CZI Cell Science Program et al. 2025), now make annotated data from many donors available for most human tissues. These collections are the natural source of cell-type profiles for the analysis of spatial data.

## 1.2 Spatial transcriptomics and the mixed nature of spot measurements

The dissociation on which single-cell sequencing depends separates every cell from its anatomical context. Spatially resolved transcriptomics was developed to preserve this context by measuring gene expression directly in tissue sections, through two broad families of technologies: imaging-based methods, which detect a predefined panel of transcripts in situ at subcellular resolution, and sequencing-based methods, which capture transcripts on a barcoded surface and measure the whole transcriptome at a resolution set by the size of the capture area (Rao et al. 2021; Moses & Pachter 2022). In the array-based approach introduced by Ståhl et al. (2016), a section is placed on a surface carrying reverse-transcription primers with positional barcodes, so that each sequenced transcript can be traced back to the location at which it was captured. The commercial implementation of this principle, 10x Genomics Visium, captures transcripts in spots of approximately 55 µm in diameter, arranged with a centre-to-centre distance of 100 µm (10x Genomics, Space Ranger documentation). At this scale, as Ma and Zhou (2022) observe, almost all sequencing-based technologies collect expression measurements on tissue locations that consist of "a few to a few dozen single cells belonging to potentially distinct cell types"; spots of 50 to 100 µm have been estimated to contain 10 to 20 cells (Elosua-Bayes et al. 2021). The profile of a spot is therefore the sum of the transcripts of all the cells it contains. Higher-resolution platforms do not remove the problem entirely. Visium HD replaces the spots with a continuous grid of 2 µm barcoded squares (10x Genomics, Space Ranger documentation), but these are usually aggregated into 8 µm bins to increase coverage, and in the first application of the platform to colorectal cancer a proportion of such bins still contained two cell types and were resolved by deconvolution (Oliveira et al. 2025).

Spot-based data are therefore spatial in two distinct senses. Geometrically, they record where each measurement was taken and which measurements are adjacent. Compositionally, each measurement is a mixture of cell types in unknown proportions. It is this second property that stands between the raw data and most biological interpretations, since statements about the location of immune infiltrates, tumour compartments or neuronal layers all presuppose knowledge of which cells each spot contains. Unsupervised clustering of spots, the approach most widely used to identify cell types in single-cell data, does not allow for such mixtures (Cable et al. 2022). The composition of the spots must therefore be inferred by other means.

## 1.3 Inferring cellular composition by deconvolution

Cell-type deconvolution addresses this inference by estimating the proportion of each cell type within each spot. The problem was first formulated for bulk RNA sequencing, where an entire tissue sample is a mixture, and a benchmark of bulk methods on pseudo-bulk mixtures showed that methods using single-cell references perform as well as the best bulk methods, that data should be analysed on the linear rather than the logarithmic scale, and that the omission from the reference of a cell type present in the mixture degrades every method (Avila Cobos et al. 2020). Spatial deconvolution inherits this framework. Most methods are reference-based: as Ma and Zhou (2022) note, deconvolution of spatial data requires cell-type-specific expression information, which is readily available from single-cell studies. Each spot is modelled as a combination of the reference profiles of the cell types, and the weights of the combination are the estimated proportions.

Within this common framework the methods differ in their statistical assumptions. Robust cell-type decomposition (RCTD) models the counts of each spot as Poisson, with a mean equal to the total count of the spot multiplied by a weighted mixture of the reference profiles, and corrects for systematic differences between the technology of the reference and that of the spatial platform (Cable et al. 2022). SpatialDWLS first identifies the cell types that are likely to be present in groups of similar spots and then estimates the proportions by dampened weighted least squares restricted to those types (Dong & Yuan 2021). CARD complements the reference with the assumption that composition is correlated across neighbouring locations (Ma & Zhou 2022). Cell2location adopts a Bayesian negative binomial formulation that estimates absolute cell abundances and is designed to resolve fine-grained cell types (Kleshchevnikov et al. 2022). At the other extreme, non-negative least squares (NNLS) simply regresses each spot on the reference profiles under a non-negativity constraint, and MuSiC, developed for bulk tissue, extends this regression by weighting genes according to their consistency across the subjects of a multi-subject reference (Wang et al. 2019). Because every one of these methods explains the spot as a combination of reference profiles, its accuracy depends not only on its own modelling choices but also on how well those profiles represent the cells actually present in the tissue. Theoretical considerations cannot identify the most suitable method, and methods must be compared empirically.

## 1.4 Simulation studies as experiments for evaluating methods

Empirical comparisons face a central difficulty: the true composition of a spot in real tissue is unknown, so that the estimates of a method cannot be checked against the truth. The general solution, established in statistics, is the simulation study, in which data are generated by a known mechanism and the methods are judged by how well they recover the quantities that the mechanism fixes. Morris et al. (2019) describe such studies as experiments whose aims, data-generating mechanisms, estimands, methods and performance measures (ADEMP) should be stated in advance, and whose results, being estimates obtained from a finite number of simulated datasets, should be reported with their Monte Carlo standard errors. These requirements are not formalities. Pawel et al. (2024) showed that a method with no expected advantage can be made to appear superior to established competitors through undisclosed choices in the design and analysis of a simulation study, and recommended the pre-registration of simulation protocols; guidelines for benchmarking computational methods likewise warn that overly simplistic simulations and unrepresentative datasets can bias the comparison (Weber et al. 2019).

For deconvolution, the known mechanism usually takes the form of pseudo-spots, built by aggregating cells drawn from a single-cell dataset according to a prescribed composition. Every method discussed above was validated in its original publication on mixtures of this kind constructed by its authors (Cable et al. 2022; Kleshchevnikov et al. 2022; Ma & Zhou 2022), so that the simulation design forms part of the evidence for each method. Independent benchmarks rely on the same strategy. In Spotless, a benchmark of 11 deconvolution methods, 63 synthetic "silver-standard" datasets were complemented by only 3 gold-standard datasets derived from spatial data at single-cell resolution and by 2 case studies (Sang-aram et al. 2024); Li et al. (2022) likewise combined simulated datasets with paired real data. In Spotless, cell2location and RCTD achieved the best overall performance, whereas a basic regression model outperformed almost half of the dedicated methods. Benchmarks do not, however, always agree: in a bulk benchmark on human prefrontal cortex with proportions measured independently by imaging, two methods that had ranked among the best on pseudo-bulk mixtures were among the least accurate (Huuki-Myers et al. 2025; Avila Cobos et al. 2020). Whether the ranking obtained on simulated data would hold on real tissue therefore cannot be taken for granted. It is an empirical question about the simulator itself.

## 1.5 The influence of simulation design on benchmark conclusions

For single-cell analysis, this question has been examined systematically by Crowell et al. (2023), who evaluated 16 reference-based simulators on 12 public datasets, under the premise that "benchmark results (i.e., the ranking of computational tools for a given task) should be the same for experimental and simulated data". The authors measured how closely the simulated data resembled the real data and then repeated two benchmarks, of 6 batch-correction and 9 clustering methods, on both versions of each dataset. The premise was only partly met. Clustering methods performed better on simulated data than on the real data from which the simulations were derived, irrespective of the simulator, and their ranking agreed poorly with that obtained on real data, whereas agreement was closer for batch correction. Realism and reliability did not coincide: muscat was among the most realistic simulators yet produced rankings inconsistent with real data, while SPsimSeq, of only moderate realism, yielded the batch-correction ranking closest to the real one. The authors concluded that "the most truthful model for real data is real data".

These findings establish that a simulator can alter the conclusions of a benchmark, but the design of that study does not reveal which aspect of the simulator is responsible. Each simulator is a complete generative pipeline that combines its own expression model with its own noise model, so a change in ranking cannot be ascribed to any single component. Moreover, the accompanying analysis code generates one simulated dataset for each combination of reference and simulator (repository HelenaLC/simulation-comparison, version available at the time of writing); the variability in the results thus arises from the use of several references rather than from repeated simulation, and the unrealism of a simulator cannot be separated from the chance variation of a single draw. Finally, the study did not consider spatial deconvolution. Identifying what drives the instability of benchmark conclusions requires varying one component of the simulation at a time, with replicate simulations to quantify uncertainty. For deconvolution, the component of most immediate interest is the expression profile assigned to each cell type, because it is the very quantity that deconvolution methods attempt to match.

## 1.6 Where cell-type expression profiles come from

Every simulator of spatial data must specify, for each gene and cell type, the mean expression from which counts are generated, and four sources of such profiles are currently available. Empirical profiles are averages computed on a real single-cell dataset and inherit both its realism and its idiosyncrasies, including those of the particular donors from which it was obtained. Parametric profiles are drawn de novo from a statistical distribution, as in the widely adopted Splatter framework, in which gene means follow a gamma distribution and differences between groups are introduced through multiplicative factors (Zappia et al. 2017); this provides full control over the structure of the data at the cost of realism, and Crowell et al. (2023) excluded such simulators from their comparison by design. Model-based profiles are estimated by a simulator such as scDesign3, which fits an interpretable statistical model to real data and can generate new single-cell and spatial data from it; scDesign3 has itself been used to simulate spots for a comparison of deconvolution methods (Song et al. 2024). The fourth and most recent source consists of generative models trained on public archives of transcriptomic data.

GEM-1 is an example of this last category. It is a generative model trained on public human transcriptomes, including single-cell data deposited in CZ CELLxGENE up to July 2024, whose single-cell version returns a mean expression profile for a specified tissue and cell type without requiring any input data (Koytiger et al. 2025). Its developers report that its predictions approach the agreement observed between independent real samples with the same metadata, and if this held generally, such models could replace real reference data in simulation. The evidence, however, is still limited. The evaluation was carried out by the developers on a model whose weights are not public and has not yet undergone peer review, and independent evaluations of other single-cell foundation models have been less favourable: scGPT and Geneformer were outperformed by simpler methods in a zero-shot setting (Kedzierska et al. 2025), and none of several deep-learning models outperformed deliberately simple linear baselines in predicting the effects of genetic perturbations (Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025). A model trained on public archives may, moreover, reproduce data it has already encountered. This form of information leakage between training and evaluation data has led to overoptimistic conclusions in many fields that have adopted machine learning (Kapoor & Narayanan 2023), and it implies that such profiles can be assessed fairly only against data made public after the training cutoff of the model.

The four sources thus embody different compromises between realism, control and independence from the data under study. Whether these differences matter for the conclusions of a deconvolution benchmark has not been established.

## 1.7 Research question and approach of this thesis

This thesis asks whether the source of cell-type expression profiles changes the conclusions of a spatial deconvolution benchmark. Two objectives follow from the argument developed above. The first concerns fidelity: how closely the profiles obtained from each source approximate real profiles of the same tissue and cell type from donors that were not used to construct them. The second concerns the sensitivity of the benchmark: whether the ranking and the error of the deconvolution methods change with the source, and which source yields the ranking closest to that obtained on pseudo-spots assembled from real cells of these held-out donors, which serve as the real anchor. The two objectives are evaluated separately because, as Crowell et al. (2023) showed, greater realism does not guarantee a more faithful ranking.

The study is a pre-registered simulation study designed according to the ADEMP framework (Morris et al. 2019), and it is constructed to overcome the limitations identified in Sections 1.4 and 1.5. Only the source of the mean expression varies between the arms of the study, while the pseudo-spot generator and the negative binomial noise model are shared, so that any change in ranking can be attributed to the expression source. The data of each tissue are partitioned by donor, so that the profiles of every source are evaluated on individuals that did not contribute to their construction. The four sources described in Section 1.6 are compared against a real anchor made of cells whose type, and hence the true spot composition, is known. Two internal controls frame the comparison: a negative control obtained by permuting gene labels within each cell type, which is expected to fail, and a reference derived from a second independent donor, which measures donor-to-donor variability and therefore provides the scale on which the other sources are interpreted, rather than an upper bound that no source could exceed, in the spirit of the pseudoreplicate comparisons used to evaluate generative models (Koytiger et al. 2025). All performance measures are estimated over replicate simulations and reported with their Monte Carlo standard errors. Because benchmark conclusions may depend on the reference dataset as much as on the simulator (Crowell et al. 2023), the analysis is repeated in three tissues of different composition: colon and colorectal cancer, lymphoid tissue and brain. The deconvolution methods compared are RCTD, cell2location and SpatialDWLS, the three best-performing methods of the Spotless benchmark, together with NNLS and MuSiC as simpler regression baselines (Sang-aram et al. 2024). CARD is not included: its advantage derives from the spatial correlation of composition between neighbouring spots, which pseudo-spots without positions do not possess, so that it could not be evaluated fairly in this design.

The answer to this question has a practical as well as a methodological purpose. It will indicate to those who design deconvolution benchmarks how much their conclusions depend on the expression source, and it will inform the choice of expression source for the next phase of the spatial simulator developed within the broader project of which this thesis is part.

## 1.8 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 describes the pre-registered study design: the data and their partition by donor, the data-generating mechanisms, the deconvolution methods and the performance measures. Chapter 3 presents the results for the two objectives and for the control arms. Chapter 4 discusses their implications for the design of deconvolution benchmarks, the limitations of the study and directions for future work.
